A regional customer advocacy board

As Uniphore entered new markets, customer referrals carried real weight with buyers, analysts, and our own product teams. I set up a five-customer advocacy board, one advocate per region, and the processes to run it.

At a glance

Situation
Uniphore was entering new markets and scaling rapidly, and needed customer advocates for sales, analyst evaluations, and product feedback.
My role
Set up the customer advocacy board and the processes to run it.
Result
10 new deals, 12 new case studies and video testimonials, and 8 analyst briefings attended by board members.

Context

Uniphore sells conversational AI to enterprise contact centres. As it entered new markets and scaled rapidly, a referral from an existing customer went a long way in establishing its reputation and reliability.

The problem

Customer advocacy wasn't systemised. Before the board, there were no processes for finding advocates, keeping them engaged, or matching them to requests from sales, analysts, and product.

My role

I owned

Setting up the advocacy board and its processes: choosing members, the monthly email, the quarterly meeting agenda, and the testimonial tiers

I worked with

Customer success, and the customers on the board

Approach: key decisions

  1. One advocate per region

    We picked one customer from each region we sold in, North America, APAC, ANZ, and EMEA, plus one global organization.

    Why: Uniphore was entering new markets, and buyers trust a reference from their own region.

  2. Make it worth their while

    Board members got early access to new features, free seats or additional consumption, a say in the roadmap, and places at conferences they wouldn't usually attend.

    Why: Advocacy works both ways. Members stayed engaged because the board gave them something valuable too.

  3. Set a steady rhythm

    I set up a monthly email to keep members up to date, and a quarterly meeting with a set agenda: product and service feedback, speaking opportunities and events, analyst calls, and requests from sales.

    Why: A regular cadence made asks for testimonials, reference calls, and analyst briefings routine rather than ad hoc.

  4. Tier the testimonials

    Customers could give a quote, a one-pager, or a long case study with video.

    Why: Customers could choose how much time to give, and sales had proof at the right depth for each deal.

What I built

Results

  • 10 new deals from board advocacy
  • 12 new case studies and video testimonials
  • 8 analyst briefings attended by board members, helping Uniphore break into Gartner Magic Quadrant and Hype Cycle reports and the Forrester Wave

What I'd do differently

I'd start the board as early as possible. We set it up eight months after I joined; starting sooner would have given sales, analysts, and product access to advocates much earlier.
